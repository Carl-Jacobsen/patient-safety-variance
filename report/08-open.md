# 8. What remains open

The largest open question is the one the design cannot reach. A single cross-section of published scores cannot tell a shortfall in risk adjustment from a real difference in the care that hospitals in disadvantaged catchments provide, so the report says how much of PSI-90's variation public community measures describe and how unsettled the direction is, not whether the score is unfair to any hospital. The stronger question needs patient-level data, such as the HCUP state inpatient databases, or a panel of reporting windows in which a hospital's own changes can be followed.

The reliability approximation reaches further than the weighted sensitivity analyses: it helps choose the main Rashomon set and shapes the payment simulation's score draws. The published-score set in section 6 and the fixed-score payment run in section 7 show what changes without it, and neither changes a direction this report states. The approximation is a single normal shrinkage, while CMS smooths each component separately and, in the AHRQ documentation for its software, computes the composite's variance with covariance terms among the components whose signs the available documents do not establish, so whether the approximation runs high or low for this release is not known. A question to the QualityNet help desk has been drafted, not sent. In the quarter of hospitals with the lowest reliability, the payment check's calibration slope against observed HACRP penalties is 1.29, outside the bar of 0.8 to 1.2 set in advance, so the simulated scores are least dependable where the published scores are least reliable.

The CASH2 Tracks do not correspond to stable groups in the national data. On star rating and operating margin, 1,591 of 2,581 hospitals (61.6%) fall outside the three cells the Tracks describe, and on PSI-90 and operating margin across 2,759 hospitals, ICL selects 2 groups on the published score and 4 on the reliability-corrected score while the gap statistic selects 1 on both, with ICL's choice recurring in only 44% and 59% of 100 random 80% subsamples. An ROI exercise built on the national file should use hospital-level inputs rather than per-Track parameters.

The direct and shared reliance method the earlier draft used to rank community measures was repaired in the second pass and now tags no measure as essential or substitutable once the metropolitan-area tolerance is applied; at the primary tolerance only the limited-English share's unique part clears the threshold, narrowly. It is reported in the supplement as exploratory, and no recommendation rests on it.

Four pieces of work are deferred past the contract. The simulation models rewards for rank but not credit for year-over-year improvement, which Elevance describes; a hypothetical credit could be simulated now, but estimating how much hospitals actually improve needs a second PSI-90 reporting window. The value of HCUP data, Leapfrog grades and a panel depends on access and on which question CASH wants answered first. The cross-term share of the shared-reliance allocation is uncomputed. And the complete-case restriction, which section 3 identifies as the missing-data assumption that matters, could be tested by imputing operating margin, uncompensated care and market concentration and refitting the 736 composite specifications.

The return-on-investment question is the next exercise. Its definition, kept with the CASH2 revision ledger, lists the inputs a hospital-level ROI needs (eligible revenue, intervention cost, achievable reduction and payment timing among them) and leaves open whether the unit should be the facility or the system; settling that, and choosing between the fairness and investment questions, are left to CASH. One check remains before handoff: the pipeline has been rebuilt from raw data on the machine that built it, but not yet on another machine from the documentation alone.
