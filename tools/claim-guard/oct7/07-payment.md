# 7. Payment consequences

The fairness question already applies to a program in force. In FY 2026, HACRP reduced Medicare payments by 1% for the quarter of hospitals with the worst scores, and hospitals in the most deprived fifth of catchments were penalized at 1.39 times the rate of the least deprived fifth (95% interval 1.14 to 1.71, hospital bootstrap); by the models' ranking of catchment disadvantage the ratio is 1.65 (1.35 to 2.02). Safety-net hospitals were penalized at 1.14 times the rate of others (0.98 to 1.33), an interval that includes parity. HACRP's score combines PSI-90 with five infection measures, so these ratios describe the program's penalties, not PSI-90 alone. They describe who was penalized across the 2,551 hospitals with a HACRP decision, not why, and they need no assumption about any payment rule beyond the one CMS publishes.

The rest of this section describes a rule that is not public. Q-HIP's formula is not available at the detail a simulation needs, so the simulation applies a Q-HIP-style rule as CASH2 specified it: a bonus of 0.1% to 4% of commercial revenue, set by a hospital's rank on a safety score in which PSI-90 carries 15% of the weight beside the HACRP infection measures. Every finding below describes that rule, under the scenarios stated, for the 2,569 hospitals in the model frame. None describes payments any hospital has received, and the dollar totals are national what-ifs, because Q-HIP participation cannot be identified by hospital. The simulation's scoring reproduces HACRP closely (replacing PSI-90 with model draws predicts 627 penalties against 629 observed, with an area under the curve of 96.7%), but that check covers the scoring the two rules share, not the Q-HIP-style rate curve or revenue base.

The quantity that matters for an incentive is the finite payment return: how much more a hospital is paid if its PSI-90 improves by 0.05 while every other hospital's score stays where it is. Under a rank-based rule that return exists only relative to other hospitals. If every hospital improves by 0.05 at once, total payment changes by $0, a property of any purely relative rule that needs no simulation to establish and carries no modeled uncertainty. A rule of this kind can reward a hospital for passing others but cannot fund improvement across the system, which would need an explicit credit for year-over-year improvement and a second reporting window to observe it.

Per dollar of revenue, the return falls more to hospitals in less deprived catchments. Its concentration index along catchment ADI is −0.058 (95% simulation interval −0.073 to −0.044), and a hospital in the most deprived fifth of catchments gains 39.23 basis points of revenue per unit of PSI-90 improvement against 47.55 in the least deprived fifth. Safety-net hospitals' return per revenue dollar is 0.62 times that of other hospitals (0.59 to 0.65). Figure 3 shows these indices beside the observed HACRP penalty rates. Most of the difference comes from the revenue base rather than from the score: the commercial share of revenue averages 0.238 in the most deprived fifth against 0.272 in the least, and 0.212 at safety-net hospitals against 0.274 at others, so a bonus paid as a share of commercial revenue gives less per revenue dollar wherever commercial patients are fewer. Per commercial dollar the gap nearly closes, with a return of 1.621% in the most deprived fifth against 1.680% in the least, so the score's distribution accounts for only a small part of it.

That finding depends on how commercial revenue is estimated, and Table 4 shows how far. The ADI index stays negative with an interval that excludes zero under every rate curve, PSI-90 weight and commercial price ratio, weakens under CASH2's flat commercial share (−0.017) and under a scorecard of PSI-90 alone (−0.020), and shows no clear direction under CASH2's rule that cuts safety-net revenue by a factor of 0.40 (−0.008, −0.021 to +0.004). The safety-net ratio stays below 1 in every scenario, from 0.35 under that factor to 0.87 under the flat share. A per-hospital view reverses the safety-net comparison (1.18 times other hospitals' return) mostly because safety-net hospitals are larger, a property of size rather than of the formula.

The pre-registered primary ranking, catchment disadvantage as the models score it, gives a result on the edge. Its index is −0.038 (−0.085 to −0.0004), so the interval excludes zero by an endpoint that lies within one Monte Carlo standard error of it, with 97.6% of draws below zero, and 8 of the 13 other scenarios show no clear direction. That is not a basis for calling the rule regressive along this ranking. Against distance from the best measured score, the return shows no clear direction (−0.035 to +0.034), and only a convex rate curve concentrates it where that distance is small (−0.048, −0.086 to −0.009), so the shape of the rate curve, a design choice, sets how much the hospitals furthest from the best score gain from improving relative to others.

Under the base rule the simulated bonus totals $5.84 billion, and $3.62 billion to $9.05 billion across the scenarios. For CASH, the observed HACRP ratios are the stronger evidence that a payment program scoring PSI-90 falls unevenly across communities today, and the simulation adds that a commercial bonus built the way CASH2 describes would distribute its incentive unevenly as well, mainly through its revenue base. Among the assumptions varied, the revenue base moved that unevenness most, the scorecard's composition moved it too, and the PSI-90 weight barely moved it; whether crediting improvement would change it is untested here.
