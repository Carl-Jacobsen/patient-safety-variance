# 2. The question and its connection to CASH2

Hospital safety scores are published and paid on as measures of what hospitals do, and one condition for a measure used that way to be fair is that hospitals are not rewarded or penalized for the communities they happen to serve. This report asks whether the variation in CMS's published PSI-90 composite, the patient safety indicator that the Hospital-Acquired Condition Reduction Program (HACRP) scores and that a Q-HIP-style commercial bonus would reward, follows the social and economic conditions of hospitals' catchments, and how firmly public data can answer that question. It asks a second question because a fair score can still sit inside an uneven payment rule: where would the money from such a rule go?

The analysis observes neither the effort hospitals put into safety nor how their scores respond to investment. An association between PSI-90 and community measures is therefore consistent with two different situations, one in which the score's risk adjustment falls short for hospitals serving disadvantaged communities and one in which care in those hospitals differs, and nothing in this report separates them. Every finding is stated as an association across hospitals at one point in time, and the community measures are averages over each hospital's Medicare catchment, so no finding describes individual patients.

The question came out of the CASH2 memo, which built the business case for safety investment on three constructed hospital archetypes (Tracks) with parameters drawn from published ranges rather than fitted to hospital data, and which named hospital-level calibration as the next step. This project calibrated some of the hospital characteristics CASH2 assumed against national data, and described how a Q-HIP-style payment would distribute across hospitals under stated scenarios. It did not recalibrate the SSI intervention, the avoided-cost model, the malpractice pathway, or how safety responds to investment, which together produce CASH2's returns. CASH2's verification script reproduces the memo's base-case net returns on the value-based purchasing pathway (4.31, 10.38 and 9.42 for Tracks 1 to 3) and its returns with the bonus removed (2.68, 5.44 and 2.92). That confirms the arithmetic under the memo's assumptions, which this project neither establishes nor overturns.

Two of the memo's Track parameters can be set against the national file. Its safety-net shares fare reasonably well: among the hospitals in the cells that match each Track's description, 47.8%, 24.3% and 11.9% are safety-net hospitals, against the memo's ranges of 50% to 70%, 15% to 30% and 5% to 10%, so the first and third estimates sit just outside their ranges and the second inside it, while the intervals around the first and third (42.2% to 53.5% and 9.1% to 15.3%) reach into them. Its bed counts (175, 300 and 500) cannot be tested this way. CASH2 describes them as scenario inputs for constructed health systems, while each unit here is a single hospital, and the median hospital in the Track 3 cell has 144 beds; the comparison is between different kinds of unit, not a failed prediction.

The community measures this report recommends are a candidate community-data module for this safety-score analysis. They are not a minimum dataset for a CASH2 business case, whose returns depend far more on eligible revenue, intervention cost, achievable reduction and payment timing than on any covariate that helps predict PSI-90. The issues in the memo that Michael's review raised, and that this project does not answer, are kept in a separate revision ledger.
