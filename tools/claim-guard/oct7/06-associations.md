# 6. Community associations across models and geography

At the primary tolerance the near-optimal models agree that the community measures carry information about PSI-90, and they disagree about what that information says. Figure 2 shows the reliance bounds. At the primary tolerance, scrambling the ten disadvantage measures raises the prediction error of every one of the 194 models in the Rashomon set (MCR⁻ 1.085, MCR⁺ 1.230), a lower bound that holds at the metropolitan-area tolerance (1.079 across 319 models) and fails at the state tolerance, where the set admits models without the community block and the bound falls to 1.000. Restricting the set to the 65 models fitted to the published score without weights leaves the primary lower bound where it was. The bounds also cover only the 736 enumerated specifications, so a richer class of models could contain one that relies on the community measures less.

Reliance says the models use the community measures, not which way the association runs, and on direction the models do not agree. Moving every disadvantage measure from its 10th to its 90th percentile changes predicted PSI-90 by −0.37 to +0.86 standard deviations across the 194 models, with 57.2% of them positive (positive means worse scores in more disadvantaged catchments), and by −0.23 to +0.18 across the 65 published-score models. In the best-fitting model at each of the four geographic grains the contrast is −0.21 (standard error 0.20) at the county catchment, +0.13 (0.19) at the tract catchment, −0.04 (0.19) at the hospital's own county and +0.09 (0.18) at the hospital service area, so no grain's best model separates the contrast from zero. That joint move describes real places, since 12% to 34% of observed catchments sit as far from the center as its endpoints at each grain.

The disadvantage measures do not move together in the models. In those four models, the part of the contrast carried by demographic composition (SVI 2, SVI 3 and the limited-English share) is negative at every grain (−0.15 to −0.29), the part carried by rurality and primary-care density is positive (+0.16 to +0.24), and the part carried by material deprivation (ADI, the income index, SVI 1, SVI 4 and the uninsured share) changes sign with the grain. A negative coefficient on the limited-English share is not evidence that limited-English populations are protected, and a positive coefficient on the minority-status theme is not a patient-level risk; both are associations between catchment averages and a hospital score, conditional on everything else in the model. Averaged over observed catchments rather than read from the models (a supplementary comparison chosen after seeing the data), hospitals in the most materially deprived tenth of catchments have PSI-90 about 0.23 standard deviations worse than the least deprived tenth (standard error 0.10 at the county catchment), and hospitals in the tenth highest on composition about 0.44 better (0.11), with the same signs at every grain.

How the catchment measures are built changes the answer. The SVI themes are ranks computed separately for tracts and for counties, so the same theme is not the same quantity at the two grains; rebuilding the themes from comparable tract quantities brings the tract and county contrasts from −0.21 and +0.13 to −0.19 and 0.00, a gap that falls from 0.34 to 0.19 standard deviations. The remaining gap comes from the income index and the limited-English share, which differ by how they aggregate rather than by how they are built. No interval was computed for the gap itself. Ownership and system membership, added to the structure block, move the contrast from −0.21 to −0.24 at the best model and from +0.14 to +0.10 at the median one.

The suppressed discharge counts matter little for the averages and a great deal for individual hospitals. Filling every suppressed cell at 10 discharges changes the best county-grain model's error by 0.12% and its contrast by 0.044, but the rank correlation of hospitals' community contributions falls to 0.924, and to 0.840 among the 251 hospitals with the fewest mapped discharges, where one hospital moves 1,696 places of 2,569. These are scenarios with no probability attached, and they show that public data do not support a hospital-by-hospital community adjustment of PSI-90.

The community measures still earn a place out of sample, and the held-out validation (Table 3) shows which ones. In states the model has not seen, dropping the community block raises held-out error by 2.2% (1.0% to 3.1% over 20 repeats). The four SVI themes with the limited-English share come within 0.37% of the full block (−0.98% to 1.34%) and fall inside the 1% equivalence margin in 90% of repeats, while ADI alone falls 1.30% short (0.11% to 2.25%). Within known areas, where the comparison is more precise, the SVI set keeps 83% of the full block's gain (73% to 97%) and ADI alone 44% (33% to 53%). All of this sits on a small base: the best model explains 4.0% of the variation in unseen states and 6.8% within known areas, against 11.0% in sample, and no interval was computed for those shares.

For the fairness question these results limit what can be said in both directions. The data do not establish that PSI-90 penalizes hospitals in disadvantaged catchments, and they do not establish that it favors them; the sign of the association depends on which measures carry it, at what grain they are built, and which of several equally good models one reads. What the data do support is narrower: a model of PSI-90 that leaves out the community measures predicts worse, and if a community-data module is kept for this analysis, the SVI themes with the limited-English share are the better candidate than ADI alone.
