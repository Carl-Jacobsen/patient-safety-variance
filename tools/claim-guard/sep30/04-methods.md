# 4. Methods

The question behind this report is how much of the variation in hospital patient safety scores tracks the communities hospitals serve rather than the hospitals themselves, and what that means for a payment program that rewards the scores. Answering it with one statistical model would invite an obvious objection, because many models fit these data about equally well and they do not all give the same answer. The methods are chosen to answer that objection directly: instead of reporting the one model an analyst happened to prefer, the report asks what every credible model agrees on. That is the idea behind Model 2 of the CASH business-case framework, which asks "which inputs are necessary across credible models and which are substitutable," and the methods here are that model applied to a dataset mature enough to support it. Table 4.1 pairs each method with the question it answers.

Table 4.1. The methods and the questions they answer.

| Question | Method |
|---|---|
| How much of the variation tracks community disadvantage, and how much tracks hospital structure? | Variance decomposition by Shapley averaging |
| Does the answer depend on the analyst's choices? | Specification curve over every registered combination of choices |
| Which models count as credible? | The Rashomon set: every model within a bootstrap tolerance of the best |
| Can any credible model do without a given covariate? | Model class reliance (MCR) |
| Is a covariate's importance its own, or shared with correlated covariates? | Direct and shared reliance (the total importance score) |
| Do hospitals fall into the three CASH2 Tracks? | Cross-tabulation against a verdict rule fixed in advance |
| Where would a Q-HIP-style bonus go? | Monte Carlo simulation of the payment formula |

Every analysis is cross-sectional and associational, describing how safety scores are distributed across hospitals and communities at one point in time. The report does not claim that a community's characteristics are the reason for a hospital's safety performance, and the language throughout says "associated with" for that reason.

## 4.1 The published score is an estimate

The PSI-90 composite that CMS publishes is not a raw count of adverse events. For each hospital CMS combines its own rates with the national average, giving the national average more weight when the hospital has fewer patients, so a small hospital's published score sits close to the national mean whatever its true performance. The reliability λ measures how much of each hospital's own signal survives this process. Because reliability is lowest at small hospitals, and small hospitals are disproportionately rural, the smoothing does not simply add noise; it shrinks differences between hospitals in a pattern related to the covariates the study examines.

The models handle this in four ways, and every one is reported. The first ignores reliability and treats the published score as exact. The second gives each hospital weight in proportion to its reliability, so that precisely measured hospitals count for more. The third restricts the analysis to hospitals whose reliability is high. The fourth reverses the smoothing: it stretches each hospital's published deviation from the national mean by the inverse of its reliability, recovering an estimate of the unsmoothed score, and then weights the result so that the stretched values of unreliable hospitals do not dominate (an approach known as an errors-in-variables correction). None of the four smooths the score a second time. The four component measures, which CMS publishes without composite smoothing, provide a separate check.

## 4.2 Splitting the variation among the blocks

The first question is how much of the variation in PSI-90 each block of covariates accounts for. The obvious method, adding the blocks one at a time and crediting each with the improvement in fit it brings, gives an answer that depends on the order of entry, because the blocks overlap: hospital wage index and community deprivation, for example, are strongly correlated, and whichever block enters first takes the credit for what they share. The decomposition used here removes that arbitrariness by computing each block's contribution under every possible order of entry (six orders for three blocks) and averaging them, a procedure known as Shapley or LMG averaging. The shares it produces add up to the share of variance the three blocks explain together, and the remainder is variation that none of the blocks accounts for. Confidence intervals come from refitting the decomposition on 1,000 resampled versions of the data. The decomposition is run on the published composite, with and without the reliability weighting, and on the component measures, where the resampling draws whole Hospital Service Areas, since hospitals within one area share part of their variation.

## 4.3 Many defensible models

Any analysis of these data involves choices that a reasonable analyst could make differently: which covariate blocks to include, whether the relationships are straight lines or curves, which outcome to model, how to handle reliability, at what geographic grain to measure the community, how to treat missing values, whether to set aside unusual hospitals, and whether to model the outcome on its original or a logarithmic scale. Rather than choose one answer to each, the analysis registered every defensible combination of the eight choices before fitting any of them, which gives 3,496 specifications, and fitted all of them. The 3,344 specifications that include community disadvantage each give an estimate of how much PSI outcomes differ between a hospital whose catchment sits at the 10th percentile of disadvantage on every measure and one at the 90th percentile. Plotting all of those estimates in order (a specification curve) shows how much the answer depends on the choices, and which choices move it. The registry was committed to the project's history before the first model was fitted, so the list of specifications could not be adjusted after seeing results.

## 4.4 The Rashomon set

A specification curve treats every specification as equally credible, which they are not; some fit the data much worse than others. The Rashomon set, named for the film in which witnesses give equally plausible but conflicting accounts of one event, is the set of models that fit the data nearly as well as the best one. A model belongs to it if its prediction error exceeds the best model's by no more than a tolerance, and the models inside it are, for practical purposes, indistinguishable on the evidence.

The tolerance is the one number that decides membership, so it was set by a procedure rather than by judgment. The best-fitting model was refitted on 1,000 resampled versions of the data, and each refit was scored against the original data. Those refits are all versions of the best model, so the spread in their errors shows how much prediction error moves through sampling variation alone. The tolerance is the 95th percentile of that spread, and the percentile was fixed in writing before any error was computed. For the published composite the tolerance works out to 4.1% of the best model's error, and 194 of the 736 composite specifications fall within it.

The resampling unit matters more than the percentile does. The primary tolerance resamples individual hospitals, which treats hospitals as independent. Hospitals in the same metropolitan area or state may share influences the covariates do not capture, and resampling whole areas gives larger tolerances: 5.1% when metropolitan areas are resampled and 9.3% when states are. The report treats the hospital tolerance as primary, because it was fixed in advance, and reports the reliance bounds at all three.

The Rashomon set is reported with its width, the range of answers its members give. Across the 194 members, the estimated difference in PSI-90 between the most and least disadvantaged catchments runs from −0.37 to +0.88 standard deviations. A conclusion that every member supports holds for every credible model, and one that only some members support does not, however well it fits the preferred model.

## 4.5 Model class reliance

Model reliance measures how much a model needs a covariate. The covariate's values are scrambled across hospitals, which keeps its overall distribution but breaks its link to everything else, and the model's prediction error is recomputed. The ratio of the scrambled error to the original error is the model's reliance on that covariate. A ratio of 1 means the model does no worse without the information, and the amount by which the ratio exceeds 1 is the proportional rise in its error. Here the scrambling is not done by random draws; the error is averaged over every possible pairing of hospitals, so the reliance values carry no simulation noise.

Model class reliance (MCR) applies that measure to every model in the Rashomon set at once. Its lower bound, MCR⁻, is the reliance of the least reliant credible model, and its upper bound, MCR⁺, is the reliance of the most reliant. An MCR⁻ above 1 means that no model which fits the data nearly as well as the best can ignore the covariate: even the model that relies on it least loses accuracy when it is scrambled. For the block of ten community disadvantage measures, MCR⁻ is 1.085 at the primary tolerance, so scrambling community disadvantage raises the prediction error of the least reliant credible model by 8.5%.

A p-value answers a narrower question than MCR⁻ does. It is computed inside one model, assuming that model is correct, and asks whether a coefficient could plausibly be zero. A different credible model, with a different set of controls or a curve instead of a line, gives a different coefficient and a different p-value, and a covariate that looks significant in one model can look negligible in its neighbor. MCR⁻ > 1 is a claim about all of those models together: whichever credible model one prefers, it needs the covariate. It is also a claim about predictive accuracy rather than about one coefficient, so it can hold even when the models disagree about the direction or size of the relationship. That is the situation with community disadvantage in these data: every credible model relies on it, and they do not agree on the size of the contrast.

MCR⁻ has three limits, and each is stated wherever the bound is used. It describes the models, not the world; it says that no credible model on these data can ignore a covariate, not that the covariate affects safety. It is computed over the specifications the analysis listed, and a richer set could contain a model that relies on a covariate less, so MCR⁻ is an upper bound on what a larger search would find. And it depends on the tolerance: at the state-level tolerance the Rashomon set admits models with no community disadvantage at all, and MCR⁻ for the block falls to 1.000.

## 4.6 Direct and shared reliance

Community disadvantage is measured ten ways, and the measures overlap heavily; ADI and the income-concentration index, for example, largely move together (in opposite directions, since a high ADI marks deprivation and a high ICE marks affluence). When measures overlap, a model can rely on one or another interchangeably, and scrambling one measure understates its value because its neighbors can partly stand in for it. The reverse also happens: a measure can appear important only because it carries information that a neighbor supplies.

The total importance score separates the two, splitting each measure's reliance into a direct part, which is the reliance on the portion of the measure that no combination of the other measures can reconstruct, and a shared part, which is the reliance on information its neighbors also carry. A flow table records, for each pair of measures, how much of one measure's apparent importance comes from information it shares with the other. A measure's total score credits it with its own direct reliance and with the part of other measures' shared importance that comes from it. The split was checked against simulated data in which the true sharing was known, before it was applied to the project's data.

This split maps onto the tags that Model 2 of the framework asks for. A measure with direct reliance above 1 in every credible model is essential in the framework's sense, because nothing else in the dataset can stand in for it. A measure whose importance is mostly shared is substitutable, because its neighbors carry most of what it contributes. Ordinary reliance and direct reliance can point in opposite directions: SVI theme 3 (racial and ethnic minority status) has a median ordinary reliance of 0.998, which taken alone would suggest a model could drop it, but its direct reliance exceeds 1 in every credible model, and it appears dispensable only because a correlated measure (the limited-English share) offsets part of its contribution.

## 4.7 Testing the Track archetypes

The CASH2 memo describes three hospital Tracks defined jointly by financial margin and safety maturity. Whether hospitals actually fall into those Tracks was tested against a rule written down before the test was run. Hospitals were cross-classified by CMS star rating (the fallback for Leapfrog grades, which are sold rather than published) and by operating margin tercile, and the rule has three outcomes. If star rating and margin are not positively associated, the Tracks are not supported. If they are associated but fewer than half of hospitals sit in the three Track cells, margin and safety form a gradient rather than distinct groups. If they are associated and at least half sit in the Track cells, the Tracks are supported as strata. For comparison, about a third of hospitals would sit in the Track cells if rating and margin were unrelated. The memo's other per-Track parameters (safety-net share, bed count and baseline safety) are each checked separately against the memo's own figures.

## 4.8 Simulating the payment

The payment analysis asks where a bonus modeled on Elevance's Q-HIP program would go. Q-HIP pays a bonus of between 0.1% and 4% of the commercial fee schedule, and the simulation sets each hospital's rate within that band by its rank on a safety score, so the dollars a hospital can earn depend both on its rank and on how much commercial revenue it has. The simulation applies that formula to every hospital, using each hospital's own net patient revenue and commercial share from its cost report, a safety score built from PSI-90 and the infection measures in HACRP, and a model from the Rashomon set to connect PSI-90 to the hospital's characteristics.

Each of 8,000 draws picks one of the 194 credible models at random, draws its coefficients from their estimated uncertainty, and draws each hospital's true PSI-90 from the range its published interval allows, so the results carry specification, parameter and measurement uncertainty together. Structural assumptions, such as the weight PSI-90 receives in the score or a threshold below which no bonus is paid, are varied separately as named scenarios, so that each result can be traced to the assumption behind it. Before any draw was made, the model had to reproduce the actual FY 2026 HACRP penalty decisions from HACRP's own scores, and it did so for 3,054 of 3,055 hospitals (the exception has no published score to reproduce).

Whether the bonus is regressive is measured with a concentration index, which summarizes whether a quantity is concentrated among hospitals ranked low or high on some other measure, such as improvement headroom or catchment disadvantage. It runs from −1 to +1, where 0 means the quantity is spread in proportion to hospitals' shares. The rule for calling the formula "structurally regressive" along a ranking, a term that here describes only the arithmetic of the payment formula, was fixed before the index was computed: the index for the incentive to improve (the added bonus a hospital would earn per unit of PSI-90 improvement, per dollar of its revenue) must be negative along that ranking, with a 95% interval that excludes zero. The term makes no claim about anyone's intent, or about what any hospital has actually been paid.

## 4.9 What was fixed in advance

The choices that could most easily be tuned toward a preferred answer were written down and committed before the analyses they govern: the admission gates for Block C, the list of 3,496 specifications, the percentile that sets the Rashomon tolerance, the clustering criterion and the Track verdict rule, and the regressivity rule. Each was committed ahead of the results it governs, so the timestamps in the project's history show the order. Where a choice was changed after the fact, the change is recorded as a numbered amendment with its reason, and where it was made after results were seen, the report says so. Two further rules govern how findings are stated in the report. Every claim about the importance of a covariate is reported with the width of the Rashomon set beside it, and every finding about catchments is stated as a property of communities, never of individual patients.
