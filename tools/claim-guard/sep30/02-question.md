# 2. The question and its origin in CASH2

The CASH2 memo made the business case for hospital safety investment at the level of scenarios, through return-on-investment models built on three constructed hospital archetypes, with parameters drawn from published sources rather than fitted to hospital data and with sensitivity analysis that varied one input at a time. The memo stated that limit in its own words: "No real hospital's data has passed through it, and the archetypes are constructed illustrations." It named the public datasets that would change that, CMS Care Compare among them, and described fitting the archetypes to those sources as "the concrete next step for CASH2." Its verification review then graded the remaining work and deferred two lines of it as beyond the scope of an internal memo, listing them as "Hospital-level calibration, real Q-HIP eligible-revenue data, or causal estimation of score-to-payment response" and as "Monte Carlo analysis, publication-grade systematic review, or independent external validation."

This report takes up the hospital-level calibration and the Monte Carlo analysis, and it approaches the eligible-revenue item as closely as public data allow. Every acute care hospital with a published PSI-90 enters the analysis with its own covariates and its own safety score, so the archetypes can be tested against the hospitals they were meant to represent. Q-HIP's actual payments and contract terms are not public, so each hospital's eligible revenue is estimated from its own cost report and payer mix, and the simulation carries the uncertainty in the models and in the published scores through to the distribution of payments. Causal estimation of how hospitals respond to payment, systematic review and external validation remain outside the report's scope, and no finding depends on them.

The question the calibration answers is whether a hospital's published safety score tracks the community it serves as well as the hospital itself. Safety scores are reported and rewarded as measures of hospital performance, but a hospital's score may also reflect differences in its patients' health, in its resources and in how thoroughly its staff document complications, and each of those may vary with the deprivation of the surrounding community. If the score does track community deprivation, a program that rewards the score rewards, in part, the community a hospital happens to serve, and the incentive reaches the hospitals with the most room to improve only if the program is designed with that association in view. Q-HIP's payment formula gives the question a specific form for CASH. Q-HIP pays a percentage of the commercial fee schedule, so a hospital's commercial share is plausibly associated with its safety performance, through the resource differences the CASH2 archetypes already describe, and it also sets the size of the incentive the hospital can earn. If the two run in the same direction, the hospitals with the most room to improve have the least incentive money with which to improve, and that property of the formula can be checked by arithmetic before any statistical model is fitted.

The analysis also answers a question the CASH business-case framework already poses. The framework's second model, on MCR-informed data prioritization, asks which variables are likely to matter most to a business case and which data elements are worth collecting, and it specifies as its deliverable "a ranked minimum viable data set for CASH business-case modeling, with each input tagged as essential, useful, substitutable, or optional." It treats model class reliance (MCR), a measure of how much every credible model depends on a covariate, as the advanced form of that analysis, to be used "once there is a dataset or mature exemplar model" in order to "identify which inputs are necessary across credible models and which are substitutable." The national hospital file assembled for this report is such a dataset, built entirely from public sources with recorded provenance, and the reliance analysis applies MCR to it. The CASH literature review found no published work applying Rashomon-set or Monte Carlo methods to the prediction of safety-event frequency, so the approach has no precedent in this field to be compared against.

The design sets four boundaries on what the report can claim. Every claim is associational, because a single cross-section of hospitals cannot separate the contribution of a community from the contribution of the hospitals that serve it, and the report says "associated with" throughout. The unit of analysis is the hospital, and community characteristics enter as averages over each hospital's catchment, so no finding describes individual patients. The data are public and require no data use agreement; discharge-level files that would sharpen several results, such as the HCUP state inpatient databases, were pursued on a separate track that the analysis does not depend on. Finally, the CASH2 return-on-investment models are tested against the data and not rebuilt, so where their inputs fail the report says so and recommends replacements, without producing a revised version of the memo.
