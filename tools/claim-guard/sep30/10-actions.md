# 10. Recommended actions

Five actions follow from the four decisions facing CASH, and each is stated with the evidence behind it and the limits of that evidence. Where an action would change a payment design, it is offered as an option to test rather than a design this report has evaluated, because the simulation varied only a small set of named features of the formula.

## 10.1 Treat the published score as an estimate

CASH's business-case models should carry each hospital's reliability alongside its PSI-90, as this project does, and should not compare small and large hospitals on the published composite as if the two were measured with equal precision. The median hospital's published score retains 46% of its own deviation from the national mean, rural hospitals a mean of 0.304, and hospitals in the lowest quarter of Medicare volume 0.205. Any ranking-based design CASH evaluates should be run twice, once on the published score and once on a reliability-corrected version, and a design whose conclusions change between the two should not be adopted on the strength of either. Reversing CMS's smoothing raised the community disadvantage estimate in 91.5% of paired comparisons in this study, so analyses that use the published score alone will tend to understate the association, most of all for rural hospitals.

## 10.2 Build the community block of the minimum dataset from the SVI themes

Model 2 of the CASH framework asks for a ranked minimum-viable dataset, and Table 6.1 supplies one for community disadvantage. The recommended core is the CDC Social Vulnerability Index file, with its four themes and the limited-English share kept as separate variables rather than collapsed into the overall index, because three of the themes and the limited-English share are the measures no credible model can replace with the others. ADI, ICE and rurality are substitutable: most of their importance is information the other measures also carry. They remain useful for communication and for checking results, but a dataset that relied on ADI alone would discard most of what the models need. These tags rest on a rule chosen after the results were seen, and SVI theme 2 holds its tag only narrowly, so the recommendation should be revisited when the next reporting window is available.

## 10.3 Invest in the two kinds of data that would narrow the disagreement

The credible models agree that community disadvantage matters to the fit and disagree about the direction of its association with PSI-90, and that disagreement follows two identifiable choices. The first is geographic grain: every credible model that weights catchments at county grain estimates a negative association, and every one that weights them at tract grain a positive one. A patient-origin file finer than the Medicare Service Area File, of which 87.46% of cells are suppressed, would help decide between them. The HCUP state inpatient databases on this project's enrichment track carry patient ZIP codes for all payers in many states, which would also remove the Medicare-only limit on the catchments where they are available. The second is reliability: the models that reverse CMS's smoothing estimate the largest associations, and more years of data or published component numerators would make that reversal more precise. The same discharge-level data would also allow a direct test of the coding objection that the partial proxy in this report cannot settle, through present-on-admission completeness and secondary diagnoses per discharge.

## 10.4 Retire the Tracks as groups, and recalibrate the per-Track parameters

The Tracks should be presented, if at all, as positions on a gradient of margin and safety rather than as three kinds of hospital, since only 38.4% of hospitals fall in the three Track cells and the middle Track does not exist as a group. The CASH2 return-on-investment models scale quality-improvement cost, procedure volume and commercial revenue from bed counts of 175, 300 and 500, and the hospitals in the Track cells have median bed counts of 151, 190 and 144. Those parameters should be replaced with hospital-level distributions drawn from the same public files this report uses, as the payment simulation already does. The safety-net shares the memo assumed are close to the data and can stay. If the archetype question remains important to CASH, purchasing Leapfrog grades would allow the test to be repeated on the safety measure the memo named, in place of the CMS star rating.

## 10.5 Test incentive designs for payer mix and thresholds before adopting them

Two features of a Q-HIP-style formula decide its distributional results, and both can be changed. The first is the threshold: paying no bonus to the lowest-scoring quarter of hospitals turns a formula that is neutral toward improvement headroom into one that offers less incentive where headroom is greatest (a concentration index of −0.076, against −0.005 without the threshold). A design that keeps a threshold should be tested against an alternative that rewards improvement for hospitals below it, which this report did not simulate.

The second is the revenue base: the formula is structurally regressive along catchment deprivation, as arithmetic of the formula, only because it pays a fixed share of commercial revenue, and hospitals in deprived catchments have less of it; when every hospital is given the same commercial share, the difference disappears. The difference is small per revenue dollar (13.92 against 15.92 basis points per unit of improvement), and the commercial-share measure used here overstates commercial share at safety-net hospitals, so the true difference is probably somewhat larger. A payer that wants the incentive to reach hospitals in deprived catchments could base part of the bonus on total revenue or add a fixed component per hospital; either change would need its own simulation before adoption.

Finally, CASH should decide which question a payment design is meant to answer. Per dollar of revenue, the formula is close to neutral; per hospital, the dollars fall fourfold from the least to the most deprived catchments, mostly because the hospitals there are smaller. If the purpose of the incentive is to fund improvement where improvement is needed, the per-hospital view matters more, and a formula proportional to revenue will not deliver it.
