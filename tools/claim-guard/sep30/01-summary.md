# 1. Summary and decision points

Hospital patient safety scores differ widely, and payment programs that reward or penalize those scores treat the differences as differences in hospital performance. This report asks how much of the variation in the CMS PSI-90 composite across 2,801 acute care hospitals tracks the communities the hospitals serve rather than the hospitals themselves, which covariates any credible model of the score needs, whether the CASH2 hospital Tracks describe real groups of hospitals, and where an incentive modeled on Elevance's Q-HIP would send its money. Every finding is an association in a single cross-section of public data, and none is a claim about why any hospital's safety performance is what it is. In brief, community disadvantage and hospital structure are each associated with a small share of the variation, every credible model needs information about communities while disagreeing about its direction, the Tracks describe a gradient rather than groups, and a Q-HIP-style formula is close to neutral toward improvement headroom per revenue dollar but offers less where catchments are more deprived, because it pays on commercial revenue.

Under the reliability-corrected model, community disadvantage is associated with an estimated 3.6% (95% interval 2.8% to 5.4%) of the variation in published PSI-90 across hospitals, against 4.3% (3.3% to 6.8%) for hospital structure; of the 9.1% that the three covariate blocks explain together, community disadvantage accounts for 39.9% and structure for 46.9%. The larger quantity is the remainder, since about 91% of the variation is associated with none of the covariates. A program that ranks hospitals on this score therefore ranks them mostly on variation that neither hospital nor community characteristics explain. Part of that remainder is measurement noise that survives CMS's smoothing and part is hospital practice and case mix that no public file records, and the analysis cannot separate the two.

Every model that fits the data nearly as well as the best one relies on community disadvantage: scrambling the ten disadvantage measures raises even the least reliant model's prediction error by 8.5%. The same models disagree about the size and direction of the association, which runs from −0.37 to +0.88 standard deviations across them, and the disagreement follows the geographic grain at which community is measured. The reliance result holds at two of the three tolerances tested and fails at the widest, which admits models with no disadvantage measure at all. One outcome gives a clear answer, since every credible model associates more deprived catchments with higher rates of perioperative pulmonary embolism and deep vein thrombosis (PSI-12).

For data prioritization, the measures that no credible model can replace with the others are three of the four Social Vulnerability Index themes (racial and ethnic minority status, housing type and transportation, and household characteristics, the last only narrowly) and the limited-English share, all from one public file. ADI, a measure often used on its own, is substitutable: 78.5% of its apparent importance is information other measures also carry. A minimum dataset built on ADI alone would therefore keep a substitutable measure and drop the four that no credible model can replace. The tags follow a rule chosen after the results were seen, so they summarize the evidence rather than test it.

The CASH2 Tracks describe a gradient of margin and safety, not three groups of hospitals. Better-resourced hospitals do tend to have better safety ratings, and the safety-net mix falls across the Tracks much as the memo assumed (48%, 24% and 12%), but only 38.4% of hospitals sit in the three Track cells, against the 50% fixed in advance as the test, and the middle Track does not exist as a group. The memo's bed counts (175, 300 and 500) do not appear in the data, where median beds run 151, 190 and 144. Because the CASH2 return-on-investment models scale cost, volume and revenue from those bed counts, their per-Track parameters describe hospitals of a size the Track cells do not contain.

Measured as the incentive to improve per dollar of a hospital's revenue, a Q-HIP-style formula sends money neither toward nor away from the hospitals with the most room to improve (a concentration index of −0.005, from −0.013 to +0.004), but it sends money away from them if the lowest-scoring quarter of hospitals earns no bonus (−0.076). It is structurally regressive along catchment deprivation, as a matter of the formula's arithmetic and by a small margin (13.92 against 15.92 basis points of revenue per unit of improvement), entirely because it pays on commercial revenue. In dollars per hospital the gap is fourfold, and 75.0% of it reflects hospital size. The program running today shows a related pattern: HACRP penalized hospitals serving the most deprived fifth of catchments at 1.39 times the rate of those serving the least deprived fifth (1.14 to 1.71).

## Decision points for CASH

The first decision is how to treat the safety score in any business case or incentive design. Because roughly 91% of the variation in PSI-90 is unexplained, and the published score is smoothed most heavily for small and rural hospitals, CASH should decide whether its models rank hospitals on the published composite at all, or on a measure corrected for reliability, before any payment calculation rests on it. The second decision is which community data to collect and maintain. The evidence favors the Social Vulnerability Index file with its themes kept separate over ADI alone, and it identifies two investments that would narrow the credible models' disagreement most: patient-origin data finer than the Medicare Service Area File, and more years or component numerators of the safety measures.

The third decision is whether to keep the Track archetypes in the CASH2 models. The test supports them as shorthand for a gradient and not as groups, and their bed-scaled parameters fail, so the alternative is to model hospitals individually, as the payment simulation does. The fourth decision is how an incentive design should handle payer mix and thresholds. Paying on commercial revenue decides the deprivation result, and withholding bonuses from the lowest-scoring quarter decides the headroom result; both are choices in the formula rather than facts about hospitals, so a payer can change them.
