# 8. Where a Q-HIP-style incentive would go

The question is whether a payment design like Elevance's Q-HIP sends incentive money toward or away from the hospitals with the most room to improve, and toward or away from hospitals serving disadvantaged communities. The answer depends on how the money is counted. Measured as the incentive to improve per dollar of a hospital's revenue, the formula is neutral with respect to improvement headroom and with respect to the disadvantage ranking the models produce, and it is structurally regressive along catchment deprivation measured by ADI, a statement about the arithmetic of the formula rather than about anyone's intent or any payment a hospital has received. That difference comes entirely from payer mix: hospitals in deprived catchments have less commercial revenue, and the formula pays in proportion to commercial revenue. Measured per hospital, the gap along disadvantage is much larger, and most of it reflects hospital size rather than the formula. Every payment result is a national what-if built on the CASH2 mechanics, not an estimate of what Q-HIP pays, since Q-HIP's actual payments are not public.

## 8.1 The mechanism is arithmetic

The simulated bonus is the product of two parts. A hospital's eligible revenue is its net patient revenue multiplied by its commercial share, measured as the non-public share of its inpatient days in its own cost report. Its bonus rate is set between 0.1% and 4% of that revenue by its rank on a safety score, in the way CASH2 modeled Q-HIP. The incentive to improve (the added bonus a hospital would earn from an improvement in PSI-90, scaled to one full unit of relative improvement as CASH2's marginal rule does) is therefore a fixed percentage of its eligible revenue, 0.585% for every hospital. Per dollar of commercial revenue, every hospital faces exactly the same incentive. Per dollar of total revenue, a hospital's incentive is proportional to its commercial share, and nothing else about the hospital enters.

That is the payer-mix mechanism this study set out to test, and it is a property of the formula that holds whatever the safety data show. Commercial share averages 0.212 at safety-net hospitals against 0.274 at others, and 0.238 in the most deprived fifth of catchments by ADI against 0.272 in the least deprived. Any payment rule that pays a fixed percentage of commercial revenue will offer less incentive per revenue dollar to hospitals whose patients are less often commercially insured, and those hospitals are concentrated in more deprived catchments.

## 8.2 Checking the simulation against HACRP

Before any simulated payment was trusted, the simulation had to reproduce a program whose outcomes are public. The FY 2026 Hospital-Acquired Condition Reduction Program penalizes the quarter of hospitals with the worst scores on a closely related safety score. Recomputed from HACRP's own published inputs, the simulation's scoring reproduced the penalty decision for 3,054 of 3,055 hospitals; the exception had no published score. Using the Rashomon models' predictions of PSI-90 instead, it expected 627 penalties among the 2,551 hospitals in the calibration sample, against 629 observed, and it ranked penalized above unpenalized hospitals correctly 96.7% of the time. The infection measures alone, which the models do not predict, achieve 87.9%, so the models recover most of what PSI-90 adds to the penalty decision. The one check that fell outside its preset bar was the calibration slope among the least reliable quarter of hospitals, 1.28 against a range of 0.8 to 1.2, which means the simulation is somewhat less decisive about small hospitals than their observed penalties were, and the model was not adjusted to close that gap.

## 8.3 Where the dollars go

Across the base scenario the simulated bonus totals $5.84 billion a year, distributed as Figure 7 shows. The bonus rate barely moves across catchment deprivation: hospitals in the least deprived fifth of catchments earn 2.05% of eligible revenue and those in the most deprived fifth 1.93%. The dollars differ a great deal: the average hospital in the least deprived fifth receives $3.33 million and the average hospital in the most deprived fifth $0.85 million, roughly a quarter as much, so that the most deprived fifth of catchments receives 7.4% of the bonus dollars while holding 20% of the hospitals. The model-implied disadvantage ranking shows the same pattern with wider intervals ($3.11 million against $1.08 million).

Most of that fourfold gap is not the formula's doing. Splitting the gap between the most and least deprived fifths into its parts, 75.0% reflects hospital size (average net patient revenue of $219 million in the most deprived fifth against $612 million in the least), 16.9% reflects commercial share, and 8.1% reflects the hospitals' positions in the safety ranking. The hospitals serving the most deprived catchments receive less mainly because they are smaller, and only secondarily because their revenue is less commercial.

Safety-net status, as the flag defines it, is not where the gradient shows. Safety-net hospitals are larger on average, which offsets their lower commercial share, and they receive 25.6% of the dollars while making up 24.6% of the hospitals. The flag and the catchment measures describe different groups of hospitals.

Along the star-rating and margin cells used to test the Tracks, the rate follows the rating, as a pay-for-performance design intends: hospitals with 1–2 stars and thin margins earn 1.41% of eligible revenue, against 2.46% for hospitals with 4–5 stars and comfortable margins. The 139 hospitals in the payment sample without a star rating earn a rate near the top (2.36%), most likely because CMS's smoothing pulls their small-sample scores toward the national mean and away from the worst ranks, though their dollars are small.

The simulation can also ask how much of each hospital's bonus the models associate with its catchment, by comparing its bonus with the bonus it would earn with a median catchment. The difference runs from +$167,000 a hospital in the least deprived fifth to −$29,000 in the most deprived fifth. Under these models, hospitals in less deprived catchments are associated with more bonus than their performance net of catchment would earn, and hospitals in more deprived catchments with less. This is associational, and it rests on models whose disadvantage contrast straddles zero, but the payment rule turns it into dollars with a consistent sign.

## 8.4 Is the formula regressive?

The rule was set out before the index was computed: the formula is called structurally regressive along a ranking, as a statement about the arithmetic of the incentive formula and not about intent or realized payments, only if the concentration index of the incentive to improve per dollar of revenue is below zero with a 95% interval that excludes zero. Figure 8 shows the indices for each ranking and scenario.

Against improvement headroom, the index is −0.005 (−0.013 to +0.004), and the formula is neutral: hospitals with more room to improve are offered about the same incentive per revenue dollar as hospitals with less. Against the models' disadvantage ranking, the pre-registered primary ranking, the index is −0.014 (−0.037 to +0.007), also neutral. That ranking orders catchments by how strongly the models associate them with worse PSI-90, which is not the same as deprivation, because the limited-English share runs against it in every credible model.

Against catchment deprivation measured by ADI, the secondary ranking, the index is −0.033, and by the rule the formula is structurally regressive along ADI, meaning only that the arithmetic of the formula offers less incentive per revenue dollar where catchments are more deprived. Two cautions apply to the verdict along ADI, one about its size and one about its precision. The index is small: a hospital in the most deprived fifth of catchments is offered 13.92 basis points of revenue per unit of improvement, against 15.92 in the least deprived fifth. And its interval has no width, because neither ADI nor a hospital's incentive per revenue dollar varies across the simulation's draws; the absence of an interval reflects the absence of modeled uncertainty, not certainty. Safety-net hospitals are offered 0.77 times as much incentive per revenue dollar as other hospitals, about 23% less, and both results come entirely from commercial share.

The verdicts depend on two design choices, the threshold and the commercial-share assumption. If the lowest-scoring quarter of hospitals earns no bonus, the variant that CASH2's own mechanics imply (three-quarters of participating hospitals earning a bonus), the formula becomes structurally regressive, as arithmetic of the formula, along headroom (−0.076, from −0.090 to −0.062) and along the models' disadvantage ranking (−0.046, from −0.078 to −0.016), because the hospitals with the most room to improve are the ones below the threshold. The commercial-share assumption, the second choice, decides the ADI verdict. With CASH2's flat commercial share, every hospital has the same share, the index is zero along every ranking by construction, and the difference along ADI disappears, which confirms that payer mix is its whole source. Adding CASH2's safety-net factor, which sets safety-net hospitals' incentive at 0.40 times that of other hospitals, shifts the incentive slightly toward deprived catchments by ADI (+0.013, again with no width) and toward the models' disadvantage ranking (+0.027, from +0.002 to +0.046), while making the formula structurally regressive, as arithmetic, along headroom (−0.009, from −0.015 to −0.004). Holding PSI-90 at its published value instead of drawing it also makes the formula structurally regressive along headroom, as arithmetic and by a very small margin (−0.004, with no width). The remaining scenarios, which change PSI-90's weight in the score, the shape of the payment curve or the measures in the score, leave every verdict unchanged.

Per hospital rather than per revenue dollar, the concentration is stronger: −0.159 (−0.228 to −0.085) along the models' disadvantage ranking and −0.203 along ADI, while along headroom it is slightly positive (+0.020, from +0.002 to +0.039). Safety-net hospitals, being larger, receive 1.25 times as much per hospital as others. Per-hospital figures describe which hospitals the money reaches and so matter for whether an incentive can fund improvement where it is needed, but they mostly measure hospital size, and they are not the basis for the verdict.

## 8.5 The program already running

The Medicare program operating today offers a comparison that needs no simulation. In FY 2026, hospitals serving the most deprived fifth of catchments by ADI were penalized under HACRP at 1.39 times the rate of hospitals serving the least deprived fifth (1.14 to 1.71), and by the models' disadvantage ranking at 1.63 times (1.33 to 1.99). Safety-net hospitals were penalized at 1.14 times the rate of other hospitals (0.98 to 1.33), an interval that includes parity. Along the star-rating and margin cells, 50.4% of hospitals with 1–2 stars and thin margins were penalized, against 11.1% of those with 4–5 stars and comfortable margins. These figures describe who was penalized, not why, and they are associations.

## 8.6 The answer

A Q-HIP-style design, as CASH2 modeled it, offers roughly the same incentive to improve per revenue dollar whatever a hospital's room to improve, so on the question this report set out to answer it sends incentive money neither toward nor away from headroom, unless it withholds bonuses from the lowest-scoring quarter, in which case it sends it away. It is structurally regressive along catchment deprivation, as arithmetic of the formula and by a small margin, because it pays on commercial revenue. In dollars per hospital, the money goes mostly to larger hospitals in less deprived catchments, which is a matter of hospital size more than of the formula, and the Medicare program already in place penalizes hospitals in deprived catchments at higher rates.
