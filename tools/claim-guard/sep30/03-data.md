# 3. Data construction and provenance

Every hospital, covariate and outcome in this report comes from a public file whose address, retrieval date and hash are recorded in the project's provenance table (`provenance/sources.csv`), and every number in the report is regenerated from those files by a single driver script (`make.R`). The table holds 33 sources, three of which were considered and not taken; they stay in the table with the reason, so that a reader can see what was tried and rejected as well as what was used. Two features of the construction matter most for the findings: the outcome is a smoothed estimate whose reliability varies with hospital volume, and the community covariates are averages over each hospital's Medicare catchment rather than values for the county the hospital sits in. The assembled file was hashed and frozen before the first model was fitted, and every analysis reads that frozen file and nothing else.

## 3.1 The sample

The sample is every hospital that Care Compare lists as an Acute Care Hospital with a numeric PSI-90 composite for the reporting window of July 2022 through June 2024, which gives 2,801 hospitals. The 107 Veterans Affairs hospitals in the same file also carry a published PSI-90, but they are not paid under the inpatient prospective payment system and are absent from the payment and cost-report files the covariates come from; they are excluded by an explicit filter and counted, rather than left to fall out silently at a later join, and no other hospital is dropped. Each of the 2,801 was matched to its Medicare provider number, ZIP code, county and census tract, and the 361 hospitals that failed one of those joins or needed a coarser fallback are listed individually in `output/tables/unmatched-hospitals.csv` and kept. Almost all of them (347) are hospitals whose street address the Census geocoder could not place, which were assigned the census tract covering most of their ZIP code's land area instead, with the fallback flagged.

The four component measures that serve as secondary outcomes do not cover the whole sample, because CMS suppresses a component when a hospital's at-risk population is too small. PSI-06 (iatrogenic pneumothorax) is published for all 2,801 hospitals, PSI-03 (pressure ulcer) for 2,778, PSI-12 (perioperative pulmonary embolism or deep vein thrombosis) for 2,727, and PSI-13 (postoperative sepsis) for 2,313. Only 2,294 hospitals report all four, and requiring all four would silently remove 507 hospitals that are systematically small and rural, which ties their absence to the community characteristics the study is about. Each component is therefore analyzed at its own coverage, and the joint count is reported rather than imposed.

## 3.2 The outcome

The primary outcome is the published PSI-90 composite, taken from CMS's six-decimal release rather than the rounded value on the Care Compare page (the two differ by rounding only). A second official PSI-90 for the identical window exists in the FY 2026 Hospital-Acquired Condition Reduction Program (HACRP) file. The two are not the same number: across the 2,793 hospitals that appear in both, they correlate at 0.9996 but differ by a mean of 0.00191, most likely because the two releases froze their claims data on different dates (an explanation CMS has not confirmed). The Care Compare value is the analytic outcome throughout, and the HACRP value is used only to calibrate the payment simulation against the penalties HACRP actually assessed. Hospital values checked against the HACRP file will therefore differ slightly from the values used here.

CMS does not publish the composite as observed events against expected events. It publishes a smoothed estimate, in which each hospital's rate is pulled toward the national mean in proportion to how little data the hospital contributes, and it publishes neither the observed numerator nor the expected rate that would allow the smoothing to be undone. The published confidence intervals do, however, reveal how hard each hospital was pulled. From them we recover a reliability for each hospital, written λ (lambda): the share of the hospital's own deviation from the national mean that survives into the published number. The median hospital retains 46% of its deviation (λ = 0.462). Figure 1 shows how strongly reliability tracks volume: hospitals in the lowest quartile of Medicare discharges retain a mean of 0.21 and those in the highest quartile 0.76, and the median rural hospital retains 0.27 against 0.51 and 0.54 in large and smaller urban areas. Because volume is itself associated with the covariates the study examines, the smoothing is not neutral noise; it shrinks differences between hospitals most where hospitals are small, which is disproportionately where catchments are rural.

## 3.3 The covariates

The covariates fall into three blocks, of which Block A describes the hospital itself through 11 covariates (beds, average daily census, occupancy, residents per bed, teaching status, case mix index, Medicare volume, disproportionate share percentage, wage index, operating margin and uncompensated care), drawn from the CMS inpatient payment Impact Files and the Medicare cost reports. Ownership and system membership were also assembled, from the Provider of Services and enrollment files, but do not enter the models. Block B describes the communities the hospital serves: the four themes of the CDC Social Vulnerability Index (socioeconomic status, household characteristics, racial and ethnic minority status, and housing type and transportation), the Area Deprivation Index (ADI), the Index of Concentration at the Extremes (ICE, the share of affluent households minus the share of poor households), the uninsured and limited-English shares, rurality, primary-care physician density and hospital market concentration. Block C carries three covariates that address rival explanations rather than the equity question: coding intensity, staffing instability and the state tort environment.

Three sources were considered during assembly and not taken. RAND Hospital Data is paywalled, and the public CMS cost report file it cleans covers 99.9% of the sample, including the contract labor line Block C needs. The Neighborhood Atlas's ZIP+4 crosswalk cannot be joined to the five-digit ZIP codes the catchment file uses, and the Atlas itself advises against computing ADI for ZIP-shaped areas, so ADI enters instead by an exact roll-up from block groups to census tracts. The HUD ZIP code crosswalk now requires an account and an API token, and the free Census 2020 relationship files do the same job.

## 3.4 Catchment weighting

Assigning each hospital the characteristics of the county it sits in is the easiest way to build Block B and the easiest to attack, because a hospital's patients rarely come from its own county alone. Every Block B covariate is instead a catchment-weighted average. The CMS Hospital Service Area File reports, for each hospital, its Medicare discharges by patient ZIP code; those discharges become weights, the weights are carried from ZIP codes to census tracts in proportion to where each ZIP's population lives, and each hospital's covariate is the discharge-weighted average of the tract values. The weights reflect a hospital's Medicare fee-for-service patients only, not its commercial or Medicaid patients.

The file has a larger problem than its Medicare scope. CMS suppresses any cell with fewer than 11 discharges, and 996,700 of the file's 1,139,665 hospital-by-ZIP cells (87.46%) are suppressed. The weights are therefore computed over the observed cells and rescaled to sum to one. The loss of information is much smaller than the share of suppressed cells suggests, because suppression removes the many small cells at the edges of a catchment and keeps the few large ones at its center. The median hospital's observed cells account for an estimated 83% of its discharges, and 81% of its catchment weight sits in its ten largest ZIP codes. We tested directly whether dropping the suppressed cells biases the covariates, by recomputing the catchment-weighted SVI of 2,730 hospitals with each suppressed cell filled in at 3 discharges (a realistic size, since few suppressed cells cross the threshold in the following year's file); the test was run at county grain, where it is cheapest to compute. The two versions correlate at r ≥ 0.986 on all four SVI themes, with mean differences under 0.004 percentile points. Catchment weighting is thus a discharge-weighted average over the observed part of each catchment, and the measured cost of the suppression is small.

Some hospitals have too little observed data for a catchment to be defined. A hospital needs at least 3 observed ZIP cells and 100 observed discharges for its catchment to be used; 130 hospitals (4.64%) fall short, 54 of them with no observed cell at all, and these take the value of their own county instead, with the substitution flagged. They are not disproportionately rural, since the rule turns on Medicare volume and small urban hospitals near a large competitor fail it too, and one arm of the specification space sets them aside.

The grain of the weighting matters more than the suppression does. Table 3.1 compares four versions of each covariate: the base case averaged over census tracts, the same weights averaged over counties, the naive value of the hospital's own county, and an average over the hospital's Dartmouth Hospital Service Area, which uses no discharge data and is therefore free of suppression. Weighted at county grain, the catchment covariates correlate with the naive county value at 0.92 to 0.97 for the SVI themes, so a county-grain catchment adds almost nothing to naive assignment. At tract grain the same correlations fall to between 0.56 and 0.88, and they are lowest for housing and transportation, the theme that varies most within a county. The tract-grain values also agree with the suppression-free Service Area version at 0.82 to 0.94 for the SVI themes, which indicates that the suppression handling is not what moves them. Tract grain is the base case for that reason, and all four grains remain an axis of the specification space.

Three construction details affect how individual covariates should be read. ADI is published for block groups, so each tract's value is the population-weighted average of its block groups' national percentile ranks, which makes the catchment ADI a population-weighted rank of ranks rather than an ADI in the Atlas's own sense; suppression removes a median of 1.3% of the block groups in a catchment, almost all of them group quarters such as prisons, dormitories and nursing homes. Connecticut replaced its eight counties with nine planning regions in 2022, and every Block B source uses the new geography while the ZIP-to-tract relationship file uses the old; every Connecticut tract was matched exactly by its unchanged six-digit tract code, without which all 25 Connecticut hospitals would have been averaged over only the out-of-state part of their catchments. The one source that could not be bridged is the physician file, so the Connecticut hospitals take the statewide primary-care density. Finally, market concentration is computed from the Service Area File itself, and because suppression removes small competitors from the count, it overstates concentration.

## 3.5 Admission tests

Block A and Block B are standard, public and nearly complete. Block C is not, and before any model was fitted each Block C covariate had to pass numeric admission gates written down in advance (`provenance/admission-gates.md`), alongside gates for catchment coverage and for the outcome sample. Of the 23 gates, 22 met their thresholds and none of the covariates was excluded; Table 3.2 gives the results by covariate.

Table 3.2. Admission test results by covariate.

| Covariate | Gates | Result | Disposition |
|---|---|---|---|
| Coding intensity (case mix index level and drift) | 5 | Case mix index present for 99.89% of hospitals; drift computable for 99.82%, with a standard deviation of 0.0527 | Admitted, and labeled a partial proxy wherever it appears |
| Staffing instability (contract labor share) | 5 | Computable for 95.04% after recovery from raw cost reports; no blank ever read as zero; hospitals without a value are smaller (standardized difference in beds +0.68) and more often rural (−0.20) | Admitted conditionally, with missing values multiply imputed |
| State tort environment | 3 | All 51 states matched; 50 distinct reform profiles; database ends 2.5 years before the middle of the outcome window | Admitted as context only; never ranked and never given an importance bound |
| Catchment coverage | 5 | 130 hospitals (4.64%) on the county fallback; median observed coverage 0.829 | Passed |
| Outcome sample | 5 | 107 VA hospitals excluded and counted; joint component sample 2,294 stated; the two official PSI-90 values differ by a mean of 0.00191 | Passed |

Two of these dispositions need more than a table row. Case mix index is an imperfect answer to the objection that hospitals serving disadvantaged communities look less safe only because they document more thoroughly or because their patients are sicker than risk adjustment captures; it cannot separate thorough documentation from sicker patients, and the report calls it a partial proxy for that reason. The one gate that failed is the balance test on contract labor share. The public CMS cost report file supplies the share for 90.7% of hospitals, and recovering values from the raw cost report worksheets and from the previous year's report raised that to 95.0% using observed values only. The hospitals still missing differ from the rest on size, so the covariate was admitted under an amendment made before any result was seen: its 158 missing values (139 never reported and 19 outside the plausible range of zero to one-half) are imputed 20 times by predictive mean matching, and every specification in the multiple-imputation arm pools the 20 versions. Contract labor share, like the rest of Block C, carries no importance bound in the reliance analysis.

## 3.6 Missing values

Of the 30 analytic variables, 14 are fully observed, including the published PSI-90, PSI-06, reliability and every Block B covariate at its base grain, and 8 others are missing for only two or three hospitals, while the remaining 8 have substantive missingness (Table 3.3). In every one of them the hospitals without a value are smaller than the hospitals with one, and every one differs by more than a tenth of a standard deviation on at least one measure of community disadvantage; for most, the hospitals without a value serve more disadvantaged catchments. That pattern matters for the equity question, since dropping incomplete hospitals would discard disproportionately many of the hospitals the question is about. In each case whether a value is missing is also predicted by characteristics that are fully observed (bed count, rurality, ownership, teaching status, wage index, volume, case mix and occupancy). That condition, called missing at random, is the one under which multiple imputation gives unbiased estimates, and the missingness audit licensed imputation for all 8.

The analyses use that license for one covariate and not the other seven. Contract labor share, the one covariate whose missingness failed its admission gate, is imputed 20 times in the multiple-imputation arm of every specification, beside a complete-case arm and an arm that omits it. The component outcomes are never imputed; each is analyzed at its own coverage. The smaller gaps in Block A and Block B (operating margin, uncompensated care and market concentration) are handled by leaving the hospital out of any model that uses the missing value, and system membership does not enter the models. With contract labor share also observed, the composite models that include all three blocks run on 2,569 of the 2,801 hospitals. The hospitals left out are the small, more deprived ones Table 3.3 describes, so the complete-case frame includes proportionally fewer of them.

## 3.7 The frozen file

The merged file (2,801 hospitals by 200 columns) was hashed and pinned before the first model was fitted, and `make.R` recomputes the hash on every run and stops if the file has changed. Changing what the file records requires a dated, numbered amendment; none has been needed. Adding a derived column during analysis does not change the file, and the analyses that did so recorded it in their own provenance notes.
