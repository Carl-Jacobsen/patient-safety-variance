# 5. How much of the variation tracks community disadvantage

Under the reliability-corrected model, community disadvantage is associated with an estimated 3.6% (95% interval 2.8% to 5.4%) of the variation in published PSI-90 across hospitals, against 4.3% (3.3% to 6.8%) for hospital structure. Of the 9.1% that the three covariate blocks explain together, community disadvantage accounts for 39.9% and structure for 46.9%. Community disadvantage and hospital structure are therefore associated with similar and small shares of the variation, and most of the variation is associated with neither. The direction of the disadvantage association (more deprived catchments with worse scores) holds in most specifications, but its size is imprecise, and the credible models do not agree on it. The models that the evidence cannot tell apart disagree most along two choices, the geographic grain at which community is measured and the form of the relationship, which identifies the data that would narrow the disagreement.

## 5.1 The decomposition

Figure 2 shows the decomposition, which divides the variation in PSI-90 among the three covariate blocks by averaging each block's contribution over every order of entry. Panel A uses the published composite for the 2,569 hospitals with every covariate observed. Without any correction for CMS's smoothing, structure accounts for 4.5% of the variation, community disadvantage for 3.3%, and the Block C covariates (coding intensity, contract labor share and the tort environment) for 1.0%. Weighting each hospital by its reliability raises community's share by 0.3 percentage points, to 3.6%, and lowers structure's slightly, to 4.3%. The direction of that shift is the one the reliability analysis predicts: CMS's smoothing pulls small, often rural hospitals toward the national mean, which shrinks the differences associated with their communities more than the differences associated with their size. The shift is small, and the two versions' intervals overlap almost entirely, so the correction sharpens the comparison without reversing it.

The larger finding in Panel A is the remainder. The three blocks together account for 9.1% of the variation in PSI-90 under the corrected model, which leaves 90.9% associated with none of them. Part of that remainder is measurement noise that survives CMS's smoothing, part is hospital practice and case mix that no public covariate records, and the analysis cannot separate the two. What the decomposition does establish is that a hospital's published safety score is only weakly predictable from its structure and its community together. A payment program that ranks hospitals on this score is ranking them mostly on variation that neither hospital characteristics nor community characteristics explain.

Panel B repeats the decomposition on the four component measures, which CMS publishes without composite smoothing, dividing the variation between hospitals rather than the variation in the composite. Structure accounts for 7.5% of the between-hospital variation (3.7% to 13.9%), policy for 3.0% (−0.6% to 8.1%) and community disadvantage for 1.5% (−0.6% to 3.7%), with 88.1% unexplained. On the components, community's share is not distinguishable from zero. The composite and the components answer related but different questions (the composite is the score a reader of Care Compare sees and the one a payment program would use, while the components isolate four specific conditions), and the two decompositions are reported together for that reason.

## 5.2 Across every specification

The decomposition reports shares of variation, which carry no direction. The specification curve (Figure 3) asks the directional question: across all 3,344 registered specifications that include community disadvantage, how much worse is PSI performance for a hospital whose catchment sits at the 90th percentile of disadvantage on every measure than for one at the 10th? Each specification's answer is expressed in standard deviations of its own outcome, so that the composite and the components can be compared on one scale.

In 69.8% of specifications the hospital serving the more deprived catchment has the worse score, and the median estimate is +0.10 standard deviations. Few estimates are precise: 7.5% of specifications are positive with a 95% interval that excludes zero, and none is negative with an interval that excludes zero. The curve is therefore positive on balance, although most individual specifications cannot distinguish their estimate from zero, and the negative estimates are imprecise rather than contradictory.

Measured by how far each choice moves the estimate when every other choice is held fixed, the choices that matter most are the outcome, the reliability treatment, the grain and the functional form, in that order. On the published composite, 77.6% of specifications are positive with a median of +0.21 standard deviations. Among the components the association is specific to the condition: PSI-12 (perioperative pulmonary embolism or deep vein thrombosis) is positive in 92.6% of its specifications with a median of +0.31, PSI-06 in 84.5%, PSI-13 in 56.2%, and PSI-03 (pressure ulcer) in only 41.7%, with a median slightly below zero. Reversing CMS's smoothing by the errors-in-variables correction raises the estimate in 91.5% of the 176 composite comparisons where every other choice is held fixed, by a median of +0.34 standard deviations, so that the composite median rises from +0.18 unweighted to +0.48 corrected. Weighting by reliability without reversing the smoothing barely moves it. That pattern is further evidence that the smoothing was attenuating the association, and that discounting unreliable hospitals does not undo the attenuation. Fitting curves rather than straight lines lowers the estimate (a median of +0.02 with curves against +0.20 with lines), and curves fit slightly better, so both shapes remain defensible. Measuring community at the hospital's own county gives larger estimates (+0.27) than either catchment grain (+0.06 at county grain, +0.08 at tract grain).

No single hospital accounts for these results: removing any one hospital moves the estimate of the reference composite models by at most 0.13 standard deviations and never changes its sign. New York is the most influential state in every composite model tested: its removal lowers the estimate by between 0.13 and 0.26 standard deviations, in part because several New York hospitals serve deprived catchments and have above-average PSI-90. That removal changes the sign only for the curved composite model and the component aggregate, whose estimates were already close to zero. Both versions are reported and no state or hospital is removed from the analysis.

## 5.3 Across the credible models

The specification curve weights a poorly fitting specification the same as a good one. The Rashomon set keeps only the models whose prediction error is within the bootstrap tolerance of the best, which leaves 194 of the 736 composite specifications. Across those 194 models, the estimated difference between the most and least disadvantaged catchments runs from −0.37 to +0.88 standard deviations. The median member estimates +0.07, 57.2% of members are positive, 7.2% are positive with an interval that excludes zero, and none is negative with an interval that excludes zero. The best-fitting model itself estimates −0.36, with a standard error large enough that the estimate is indistinguishable from zero.

The disagreement inside the set follows two of the analyst's choices. The first is the grain at which community is measured: every member that measures community by county-grain catchment weighting estimates a negative association (−0.37 to −0.08), and every member that uses tract-grain weighting estimates a positive one (+0.08 to +0.88). The second is the functional form: all 16 straight-line models in the set use the errors-in-variables correction and estimate between +0.52 and +0.88, while the curved models run from −0.37 to +0.50. Tract-grain covariates diverge substantially from county-level ones, and the two catchment grains produce different answers here; a patient-origin file finer than the Service Area File would help decide which is right. The errors-in-variables members are the ones that reverse CMS's smoothing, and more years of data or published component numerators would make that reversal more precise. Those are the two data investments that would narrow the range most, which is the kind of conclusion Model 2 of the framework is meant to produce.

Two further findings qualify what the width of the set means. First, no model that omits community disadvantage altogether fits well enough to join the set at the primary or metropolitan tolerance; the best such model misses by 2.16 times the tolerance. The credible models disagree about the direction of the association, but none of them can do without community disadvantage. Second, one outcome gives a clear answer on direction: for PSI-12, every one of the 167 credible models estimates a positive association, between +0.15 and +0.49 standard deviations. More deprived catchments are associated with higher rates of perioperative pulmonary embolism and deep vein thrombosis in every model the evidence cannot rule out, and PSI-12 is the only outcome for which that is true.
